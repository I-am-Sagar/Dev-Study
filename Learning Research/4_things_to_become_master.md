### The 4 things it takes to be an expert *by Derek Muller*

Magnus Carlsen when asked about various positions from the past games, he was able to exactly tell who played that game, in what tournament and in which year. This looks like a super-human ability. Decades ago, scientists wanted to know what makes experts like chess masters special. _Do they have incredibly high IQs, much better spatial reasoning than average, bigger short term memory spans?_ It turns out, chess masters are not exceptional on any of these measures. But one experience showed how their performance was vastly superior to amateurs. 

In 1973, William Chase & Herbert Simon from CMU recruited 3 chess players - a master (M), a class A player (A) and a beginner (B). A chess board was setup with around 25 pieces in position as they might be during the game. Each player was allowed to look at the board for around 5 seconds and then they were to replicate the position on a different board. The players could take as many 5 second peeks to get their board to match. From just the first look, the master could recall the positions of 16 pieces, A player could recall 8 and B just 4. The master only needed half the number of peeks compared to A player to get their board perfect. 

But then the researchers arranged the pieces in random positions that would never occur in a real game. Now, the chess master performed no better than the beginner. They all could recall locations of only 3 or 4 pieces. This made it clear that chess experts don't have better memory in general but they have better memory for chess positions that could occur in a real game. 

What makes chess masters special is that they have seen lots and lots of chess games. And over that time, their brains have learnt patterns. So rather than seeing individual pieces at individual positions, they see a smaller number of recognizable configurations. This is called *chunking*. For example, you recognize 3.14159 as number pi, rather than a string of 6 unrelated numbers. At its core, *expertise is about recognition. And recognition leads directly to __intuition__.* If you see an angry face, you have a pretty good idea of what's gonna come next. Chess masters recognize patterns and instinctively know the best move. 

"Most of the time, I know what I have to do. I don't need to figure it out." ~ Magnus Carlsen.

To build this recognition and long term memory of an expert, it takes a long time. Malcolm Gladwell, gave a rule of thumb, that to become expert at something, you must put 10,000 hours in it. But 10,000 hours of practice by itself is not sufficient. There are 4 additional criteria that must be met. In areas, where these criteria are not met, it's impossible to become an expert. 

#### 1. Many repeated attempts with feedback

Tennis players play hundreds of forehands in practice, chess players play thousands of games before becoming masters and physicists solve thousands of physics problems. Each one gets feedback. Tennis player sees if each shot clears the net and is in or out, the chess player either wins or loses the game, and the physicist gets the problem right or wrong. 

But some professionals don't get repeated experience with the same sorts of problems. Political scientist, Philip Tetlock, picked 284 people who make their living by commenting or offering advice on political and economic trends. This included journalists, foreign policy specialists, economists, and intelligence analysts. Over 2 decades, he peppered them with questions - Would George Bush get re-elected? Would apartheid in South Africa end peacefully? Would Quebec secede from Canada? Would the .com bubble burst? In each case, the pundits rated the probability of several possible outcomes. By the end of the study, Tetlock had quantified 82,361 predictions. 

So, how did they do? Terribly! 

These people who had postgraduate and doctoral degrees in areas in which they've spent entire lives and know the best about, performed worse than if they had just assigned equal probabilities to all the outcomes. 

**Reason:** Most of the events they were asked about were one-offs. They have not had chance to go through similar experiences many times before. Even when elections happen infrequently, they happen in slightly different environments. 

#### 2. Valid Environment

A valid environment is one that contains regularities, that makes it slightly predictable. 

A gambler betting at the roulette wheel, for example, may have thousands of repeated experiences with feedback in the form of win or loss, but you would not consider them as an expert because the environment is of low validity. A roulette wheel is essentially random and so there are no regularities to be learned. 

In 2006, legendary investor, Warren Buffet offered to bet a million dollars that he could pick an investment that would outperform Wall Street's best hedge funds over a 10 year period. Hedge Funds are pool of money that are actively managed by some of the brightest minds and most experienced traders on Wall Street. One person took Buffet up on the bet, Ted Seides of Protege Partners. For his investment, he selected 5 hedge funds. So in collection, a total of 200 individual funds. Warren Buffet took a very different approach. He picked the most basic and boring investment imaginable, a passive index fund that tracks the weighted value of the 500 biggest public companies of the America, the S&P500. They started the bet on Jan 1st, 2008, and immediately things did not look for Buffet, it was the start of the global financial crisis and the market tanked. Hedge Funds could change their holdings and can profit from the market falls as well. The hedge funds stood ahead for next 3 years but by 2011, the S&P500 had pulled even. From then, it wasn't even close. The market average surged, leaving the hedge funds in dust. After 10 years, Buffet's index fund gained 125.8% to the hedge funds' 36%. 

Why did so many investment professionals with years of industry experience, research at their fingertips, and big financial incentives to perform, fail to beat the market? Well, because stocks are a low validity environment. Over the short term, the stock price movements are entirely random. So, the feedback, although clear and immediate, doesn't actually reflect anything about the quality of the decision making. It's closer to the roulette wheel than to chess. 

Over a period of 10 years, around 80% of all actively managed funds fail to beat the market average. If you look at longer periods, the underperformance increases to 90%.  Before you conclude those 10% managers actually had the skill, some 10% people by random chance should beat the market anyway. Portfolios picked by cats and by throwing darts by monkeys have been shown to do just that. 

There was an experiment carried out with rats and humans, where there's a red button and a green button that can each lights up. 80% of times the green button would light up and 20% of times the red button would light up, but randomly. But you can never be sure which button will light. The task of the subject, either rat or human, is to guess which button will light up by pressing it. For rat, if they get right - they get some food, otherwise a mild electric shock. The rat quickly learns to press only the green button and accept the 80% win rate. Humans usually pressed the green button but once in a while, they try to predict when the red light will go on. As a result, they guess right only 68% of the time. 

We have a hard time accepting average results. We see patterns everywhere, including in randomness. So we try to beat the average by predicting the pattern. But when there is no pattern, this is a terrible strategy. Even if there are pattern, you need a timely feedback to learn them. 

#### 3. Timely Feedback

Delayed Feedback also seems to be a problem for college admission officers and recruitment specialists. After admitting someone to college and after hiring someone at a big company, you may never or much later find out how they did. This makes it harder to recognize the patterns in ideal candidates. 

In one study, Richard Melton tried to predict the grades of freshmen. A set of 14 counselors interviewed each student for 45 mins to an hour. They had access to high school grades, aptitude tests, and a 4-paged personal statement. He created an algorithm that would take just high-school grades and aptitude tests. The algorithm was more accurate than 11 of the 14 candidates. Melton's study was reported alongside over a dozen similar results across a variety of different domains. If you've ever been denied admission to an educational institution, or turned down for a job, it feels like an expert has considered your potential and decided that you don't have what it takes to succeed. So it's comforting to know that the gatekeepers at these institutions aren't great predictors of the future success. 

#### 4. Don't Get Too Comfortable

So, if you're in a valid environment, and you get repeated experience with the same events, with clear and timely feedback for each attempt, will you become an expert in 10,000 hours? The answer unfortunately is No. Because most of us want to be comfortable. 

For a vast amount of tasks, we become an expert in fairly a short amount of time - like driving a car. Initially it is challenging, but after 50 hours or so, it becomes automatic. After that more time spent driving, doesn't improve performance. If you want to keep improving, you would have to try driving in challenging situations like new terrain, higher speeds or in difficult weather. 

People play guitar for 25 years but they're not an expert because they play the same songs. It's easier and more fun for them. But in order to learn and grow, you will need to practice at the edge of your ability, pushing beyond your comfort zone. You will have to use a lot of concentration and methodically repeatedly attempt things you aren't good at. 

In chess, the best predictor of skill level is not the number of games or tournaments played, but the number of dedicated hours given to serious solitary study. Players spent thousands of hours to study chess theory, their games and games of others with compositions, puzzles designed to spot tactical patterns. In chess, just like any other area, it can be challenging to force yourself to practice deliberately, and this is why - teachers and coaches are so valuable. They can identify your weaknesses and assign tasks to address them. 

So, to become an expert, you will have to practice for thousands of hours in an uncomfortable zone attempting the things you can't do quite yet.

To me, true expertise is amazing to watch. To me, it looks like magic, but it's not. At it's core, expertise is recognition. Recognition comes from the incredible amount of highly structured information stored in long-term memory.
